Working title

A forthcoming monograph

Transformative Constitutionalism, Taxation, and State-Building

Case Studies from Kenya and South Africa

Dr Daisy Ogembo

The promise

Traditional constitutions are mainly concerned with organising and limiting state power. Kenya’s and South Africa’s do more. They also guarantee rights, place sovereignty with the people, and commit the state to democratic participation and equality. Karl Klare described this as a long-term project to transform a country’s institutions and power relationships in a “democratic, participatory, and egalitarian direction”. Its effect on tax is the subject of this book.

Karl E Klare, ‘Legal Culture and Transformative Constitutionalism’ (1998) 14 SAJHR 146, 150

About the book

Why tax?

  1. Scholarship on transformative constitutionalism has followed the commitments of Kenya’s 2010 Constitution and South Africa’s 1996 Constitution into housing, health and equality. Tax has received far less attention.

  2. Tax law sits awkwardly within the transformative project. It raises revenue for public purposes, but it is built from private law concepts such as property and contract, and from technical rules of statutory interpretation. Constitutional values of fairness, participation and redistribution unsettle that body of law. The book follows how courts, tax authorities and legislatures in Kenya and South Africa have handled the friction.

  3. It is the first book-length study of the subject. It also uses tax as a test case for a wider question about transformative constitutions: under what conditions does a constitutional commitment change settled law?

The argument

Transformation can consolidate, stall or dissipate

The book treats transformation as a cumulative constitutional effect that develops over time, and identifies three interconnected pathways.

  1. I

    Transformative but

    Constitutional principles shift tax doctrine, but unresolved legal questions, gaps in implementation, or new complexities created by the judgments themselves stop the change from settling. The result is movement without consolidation.

  2. II

    Neutralised potential

    Constitutional principles chart a route to change, which is then diverted by structural limits, scarce resources, institutional failure or incoherent policy.

  3. III

    Counter-transformative

    Constitutional requirements are met in form only. Without a culture of constitutionalism to give them substance, public participation can become a box-ticking exercise, trust between the state and taxpayers breaks down, and tax policy moves against the constitution’s aims.

From the book

Questions the book takes up

  1. Kenya

    Can a tax treaty be unconstitutional?

    The challenge to the Kenya–Mauritius double taxation agreement, and the constitutional limits on how Kenya concludes tax treaties.

  2. South Africa

    Should unclear tax laws still be read in the taxpayer’s favour?

    South African courts have long resolved ambiguity in tax statutes against the revenue authority under the contra fiscum rule. The Constitution brings a competing idea of fairness, and the courts have yet to settle which should prevail.

  3. Kenya

    When does public participation on a finance bill count?

    How Kenyan courts have tested the constitutional duty to consult the public on finance bills, and the protests that followed when consultation made little difference.

  4. Kenya

    Is a delayed VAT refund a constitutional problem?

    Refunds and the right to fair administrative action under article 47 of the Kenyan Constitution.

  5. KenyaSouth Africa

    Should taxpayers pay before they argue, or argue before they pay?

    South Africa’s default is “pay now, argue later”, tempered by a statutory power to suspend payment. Kenya’s Tax Procedures Act 2015 took the reverse position, “argue first, pay later”, but successive Finance Bills have eroded it. Since the Finance Act 2025, a taxpayer must deposit 15% of the disputed tax, or give a bank guarantee, to appeal beyond the Tax Appeals Tribunal.

The author

Dr Daisy Ogembo

Daisy Ogembo is a tax law scholar at the International Centre for Tax and Development (ICTD), Institute of Development Studies. She combines doctrinal legal analysis with qualitative research. Her work covers the taxation of self-employed professionals, the ways constitutional and administrative law shape tax systems in Kenya and South Africa, and the protection of taxpayers’ rights as tax administration moves onto digital public infrastructure.
